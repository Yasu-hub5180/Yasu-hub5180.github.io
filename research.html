Research Topics

Division of Enzyme Chemistry and Engineering
(Prof. Emeritus Yasuhisa Asano)

Enzymes catalyze chemical reactions under mild conditions, in contrast to conventional chemical processes that often require strong acids, strong bases, heat, or pressure. Enzymatic reactions are environmentally benign, energy-saving, and highly efficient. In some cases, enzymes accelerate reactions by factors of 105–107, and one gram of enzyme can produce hundreds of kilograms of product.

Our research has focused on the discovery, characterization, engineering, and application of novel enzymes from microorganisms for organic synthesis and industrial biotechnology. We have investigated hydrolases, oxidoreductases, lyases, transferases, and related enzymes acting on amino acids, peptides, nitriles, nucleic acids, and synthetic compounds.

Research activities have included microbial screening from nature, enzyme purification and characterization, recombinant enzyme production, structural biology, protein engineering, and synthetic applications of enzymatic reactions.

1. Amino Acid Dehydrogenases

1.1 Phenylalanine Dehydrogenase

We extensively studied amino acid dehydrogenases involved in asymmetric amino acid synthesis. Phenylalanine dehydrogenase was identified in several microorganisms including Sporosarcina ureae, Bacillus sphaericus, and Bacillus badius. The enzyme catalyzes the reversible oxidative deamination of hydrophobic L-amino acids using NAD+ as a cofactor.

Using a coupled cofactor regeneration system with formate dehydrogenase from the methylotrophic yeast Candida boidinii, various L-amino acids were synthesized efficiently from the corresponding α-keto acids. L-Phenylalanine production reached 116 g/L.

The enzyme from B. badius was also applied to neonatal screening for phenylketonuria (PKU) and was approved by the Japanese Ministry of Health in 1992.

1.2 Opine Dehydrogenase

A novel opine dehydrogenase from Arthrobacter sp. was purified and characterized. The enzyme is an NAD+-dependent oxidoreductase acting on opines to produce L-amino acids and α-keto acids. Detailed substrate specificity studies led to the designation of this enzyme as opine dehydrogenase (ODH).

In collaboration with Prof. Rice at the University of Sheffield, the X-ray crystal structure of the enzyme was determined at 1.8 Å resolution. Structural studies have provided insights into catalytic mechanism and protein engineering.

2. D-Stereospecific Peptide Hydrolases

We discovered several novel D-stereospecific hydrolases including D-aminopeptidase, alkaline D-peptidase, and D-amino acid amidase. These enzymes selectively recognize D-amino acid-containing substrates and have been applied to the synthesis of D-amino acids and D-peptides.

These enzymes were found to share homology with penicillin-recognizing enzymes such as β-lactamases, suggesting evolutionary relationships among peptide-processing enzymes.

3. Novel Lyases Acting on Unsaturated Bonds

3.1 Aldoxime Dehydratase

Aldoxime dehydratase is a novel heme-containing enzyme discovered during studies on microbial oxime metabolism. The enzyme catalyzes the dehydration of aldoximes to nitriles under mild conditions.

We demonstrated, for the first time, enzymatic nitrile synthesis from aldoximes and clarified the role of aldoxime dehydratase in microbial nitrile metabolism. The enzyme is positioned upstream of nitrile hydratase and nitrilase in the aldoxime–nitrile pathway.

Nitrile hydratase, originally discovered during studies on synthetic polymer degradation, has become one of the most important industrial enzymes. Industrial production of acrylamide and nicotinamide using nitrile hydratase has been carried out on a large scale in Japan, Europe, China, and the United States.
